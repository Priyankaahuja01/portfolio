<Lede>Wyoming 4-H is the state's youth development program, from Animal Science to Robotics to Cake Decorating. Its digital home had grown to 76 unique pages with no logical hierarchy, so the people who needed it most couldn't find their way through it.</Lede>

My job was a digital-ecosystem revamp: scalable layout systems, accessibility compliance, and a long-term governance model that holds up as the program grows.

## Who it's for

Two very different users have to share one website, a 15-year-old chasing a statewide contest, and a 6-year-old exploring with a parent. The design had to serve both without overwhelming either.

<FourhPersonas />

<Part n="Part 1" label="The challenge" title="Navigating massive complexity">Managing a vast digital library of 76 pages across diverse domains, built on a legacy layout that relied on link-heavy indexes rather than a logical hierarchy. The result was high cognitive load for a massive, diverse audience with no clear navigation strategy to guide them.</Part>

But the deeper problem wasn't volume, it was broken journeys:

- **"Learning" and "Doing" were disconnected.** Educational project content (skills) lived apart from event participation (application), so users struggled to find rules, dates, and locations for high-stakes contests like Meat Judging or Showcase Showdown.
- **There was an accessibility gap.** The existing site lacked a robust inclusive-design layer, failing the program's core value of Equity & Inclusion for youth with different visual or cognitive needs.

### Reframing the problem

This was not just a website problem, it was a **system design problem.** Layouts were inconsistent across pages and counties, content lacked clear hierarchy, navigation relied on long lists instead of structured pathways, and actions like "Sign Up" or "Learn More" weren't embedded in context.

<Note>The platform required structural realignment, not just a visual redesign.</Note>

## Goals & objectives

- **Establish a scalable content framework**, turn 76 pages of fragmented information into a high-performance architecture that stays easy to navigate as the program grows.
- **Bridge the "learning-to-doing" gap**, create seamless journeys from project exploration (e.g. Cake Decorating) to active event participation (e.g. Showcase Showdown).
- **Empower every user group**, serve youth ages 5–18 and adult volunteers with context-specific resources, like manuals and training guides, at the exact point of need.

<Note>The key insight: users weren't struggling for lack of content, content, navigation, and actions were disconnected. What should have been a guided experience had become a process of searching, guessing, and backtracking.</Note>

<Part n="Part 2" label="Designing the solution" title="From fragmentation to logic: six pillars">I transformed the link-heavy legacy architecture into a streamlined system centered on six primary pillars, clustering the 76 pages into thematic hubs that reduce cognitive load.</Part>

<FourhPillars />

For example, the Animal Science hub now neatly houses 13 sub-pages that were previously disjointed. A **progressive-disclosure** model handles deep density, like the 12 Expressive Arts sub-projects, without cluttering the primary journey. And a clear separation between **Projects** (skill building) and **Events & Activities** (active participation) means users can always tell the difference between learning a skill and competing in it.

## A universal accessibility layer

Inclusive design isn't a feature, it's a core design value. To make the site usable by all youth regardless of background or circumstance, I made it **ADA compliant across all 76 pages**: stronger contrast, clearer typography, and alt text on every image. For a program committed to Equity & Inclusion, this was non-negotiable.

<Figure src="/work/4h/opportunities.jpg" alt="Wyoming 4-H Opportunities landing with Project Learning, State Contests, and State Opportunities" caption="Clear, high-contrast entry points into the three things youth come to do" />

<Figure src="/work/4h/state-opportunities.jpg" alt="State Opportunities cards: Horse Camp, Youth Deer Hunt, and Food Fun 4-H" caption="Opportunity cards with real photography, consistent structure, and readable type" />

<Part n="Part 5" label="Validation" title="Testing the 76-page ecosystem with its community">I launched a targeted UX survey with members, parents, and extension staff to test whether the six-pillar hierarchy and new labels actually reduced the interaction cost, and whether the density still felt overwhelming.</Part>

The survey measured four things:

- **Findability**, how easy was it to find information, and upcoming events or opportunities?
- **Information architecture**, did the menu labels and page titles make sense?
- **Content density**, did any page still feel overwhelming, and which one?
- **Task completion**, were users able to finish what they came to do, and what's the one thing they'd improve first?

## What it made possible

<FourhStats />

### What I learned

- **Information architecture is the foundation.** A logical six-pillar hierarchy is the only thing that turns link-heavy chaos into a scalable, high-performance library.
- **Accessibility is a primary component, not a bolt-on.** Integrating ADA compliance across the entire site is what makes a digital home usable by every youth in Wyoming.
- **Validation comes from the community.** A design is only successful once its users confirm it, the UX survey taught me to iterate continuously on the real needs of youth, parents, and volunteers.

### What's next

Youth success stories and photos to build community, registration countdowns and deadline alerts on the event pages, and a specialized Clover Buds mode (ages 5–8) that prioritizes visual discovery over text.
