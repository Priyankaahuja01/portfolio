<Lede>The Jackson Laboratory (Jax) breeds and ships research mice to scientists around the world. In 2022 I worked on the team redesigning how those scientists search, compare, and price mice, three linked problems on one search experience.</Lede>

The search page and the datasheet it links to had drifted apart, and key tasks, seeing a price, filtering by who's allowed to use a strain, were harder than they should be. This study covers the three pieces I worked on: mobile pricing, the usage-license filter, and realigning the search page to the design system.

<Part n="Project 1" label="Mobile Pricing" title="Mouse price, on the go">Potential buyers couldn't see pricing or critical product details in the mobile app, they were pushed to the desktop website, a fragmented experience. The goal: surface the pricing panel on mobile, clarify the distinctions between strains, and test where the “view pricing” action should live.</Part>

I mapped the journey of Ron, a scientist who just wants to check a price before reserving mice, and the gaps were obvious: every pricing question became a detour to another device.

<Figure src="/work/jackson/mp-journey.jpg" alt="User journey map for checking mouse pricing on mobile" caption="Journey mapping made the detours, and the drop-off points, visible" />

### What testing showed

I ran an unmoderated usability test (Maze via Pendo) with 8 participants across 4 tasks over two weeks. The ES Cell “view details” option landed well, price differences by location read clearly, and the genotype dropdown worked. The one snag: ES Cell pricing confused people, a file-download icon appeared with no label.

<Figure src="/work/jackson/mp-test.jpg" alt="Heatmap from the mobile pricing usability test" caption="Heatmaps showed where attention landed, and where it didn't" />

<Note>The fix was small but real: move the file-download icon inside the cell-line rows so its purpose is obvious. User satisfaction rose 35% once pricing was viewable on the phone, and PDF downloads climbed as people did more on mobile.</Note>

<Part n="Project 2" label="Legal Facet" title="A filter for who can actually use the mouse">The usage facet only narrowed search to for-profit use. Users couldn't see non-profit options, or strains that need no license at all. The goal: make For-Profit and Non-Profit findable, surface “no license required,” and keep the JAX LEAP program in view.</Part>

<Figure src="/work/jackson/lf-current.jpg" alt="The current usage filter, showing only a for-profit option" caption="The starting point: one option, and no way to see non-profit or license-free strains" />

### Two designs, and both failed

I shortlisted two high-fidelity directions and tested them unmoderated with 24 participants.

<JaxABDesigns />

<JaxABTests />

Design A placed the options up by the search bar, it confused people and closed off a future advanced-search engine. Design B put them on the page-control bar with longer copy, users gave up at the very start. Neither one worked.

### The insight, and the design that shipped

<Note>The failure was the finding. Design A communicated well through short, clear copy, even though its placement didn't. So I kept the clear copy and moved the For-Profit / Non-Profit options, plus “no license required”, into the left filter panel, where users already look for filters.</Note>

<Figure src="/work/jackson/lf-final.jpg" alt="The shipped usage filter: for-profit, non-profit, and no-license options in the left filter panel" caption="The third direction, shipped, the clarity of Design A in the place users expected it" wide />

<Part n="Project 3" label="Design system" title="Bringing the search page back in line">Older Jax pages had drifted from the design system, and the search page no longer matched the datasheet it links to. Over 12 weeks I ran a gap analysis and a now / next / later prioritization matrix, then rebuilt the search page's layout against the existing system, this was applying Jax's design system, not inventing a new one.</Part>

I moved the page heading above the search bar so users know where they are, trialed a welcome-and-marketing block and then cut it when it added nothing, and used Crazy Egg heatmaps to see that people rarely clicked the “featured strains”, so I kept only the few that earned their place. The result is a search page that finally feels like the same product as the rest of Jax.

### What I learned

- **Invest in research early, and treat a failed design as a finding.** The shipped usage filter came straight out of the two directions that didn't work.
- **Design for the user, not for yourself.** The data, not my preference, decided which featured strains stayed.
- **In a real Agile / SAFe org you move between processes constantly.** Jax went through a big reorganization while I was there; nothing is permanent, and that's where a lot of the learning was.
