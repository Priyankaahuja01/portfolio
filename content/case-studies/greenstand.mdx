## A platform built by many hands, with no shared visual language

<Lede>Greenstand is an open-source platform that tracks trees and pays the people who plant them. Growers capture photos of trees in the field. Reviewers verify those captures. Organizations fund planting and reward people with tree tokens.</Lede>

Its tools were built by volunteer teams with no shared visual language, so every product looked and behaved differently. Reviewers moving between tools had to learn a new interface each time, and engineers rebuilt the same parts again and again.

### Color tokens with defined roles

Each color has a job. Sprout is the main action color; Sunrise and Sunset are limited to small accents so they never compete with it.

<ColorTokens />

### Accessibility decided once, in the tokens

I tested every text and background pairing and sorted them into three groups: safe for all text sizes, safe for large text only, and never. Contributors don't have to run contrast checks, if they use an allowed pairing, the result meets WCAG.

<Note>White text on Sprout looks right but fails contrast, so it sits in the "never" group. Charcoal on Sprout is the allowed version.</Note>

<Figure src="/work/greenstand/brand-contrast-matrix.jpg" alt="Typography-on-color matrix showing pairings suitable for all text sizes, large text only, and not suitable" caption="Contrast matrix: all sizes, large text only, never" />

### A 12-step type scale

Montserrat carries headings and Lato carries body and interface text. Every style lists size, tracking, line height and paragraph spacing, so engineers can implement it without guessing.

<TypeScale />

<Figure src="/work/greenstand/brand-type-scale.jpg" alt="Type scale specimen from the Greenstand style guide" caption="Type scale as documented in the style guide" />

### Elevation and borders

Two shadow levels (Y 5px / 14px blur, and Y 2px / 6px blur, both at 5% opacity) and a single 1px divider at rgba(0,0,0,.125). Fewer choices meant screens built by different people still looked like one product.

<Figure src="/work/greenstand/brand-elevation.jpg" alt="Drop shadow, lines and border specifications" />

### From brand foundations to Roots

These foundations became the starting point for Roots, the product design system the team built next. Roots kept Midnight for text and Sunrise and Sunset as rare accents. It moved the main action color to a darker green, because white text on Sprout failed contrast, the same pairing the contrast matrix had already ruled out.

<Part n="Part 4" label="Token Wallet · Jun – Nov 2024" title="A mobile wallet for earning, sending and redeeming tree tokens">There was no central place to manage and send tree-planting tokens, so businesses and individuals couldn't use them as rewards. The goal was a mobile app that makes sending tokens as easy as sending money, so more tokens circulate and more trees get planted. I was the lead product researcher and designer, covering primary and secondary research, the research plan, usability testing, wireframes and prototypes.</Part>

### Who it's for: Sam, a restaurant owner

Sam wants to reward loyal customers with trees: good for the planet and good for his brand. Today he has limited loyalty options, can't track impact, and finds giving out rewards slow.

<blockquote>"I want a simple way to show my customers I appreciate their business while also making a positive impact on the planet."</blockquote>

### The whole app, end to end

Beyond the core send flow, I designed the full wallet app. For first-time users: splash, onboarding, sign-up, account creation, a first-time home tour, notifications and creating a wallet. For returning users: log in and password reset. Then wallets with limits, details, activity and filters, plus sending and requesting tokens by name or QR code, including empty states when a name isn't found.

<Figure src="/work/greenstand/wallet-flowmap.jpg" alt="Figma overview of the full Token Wallet app: first-time and returning user flows, home, wallets, send and request tokens, search and QR code screens" caption="Full flow map from the Figma file" />

### What usability testing showed, and what changed

Participants worked through the main tasks while thinking aloud, and I logged where they struggled.

1. Users wanted a text label on every button, not only icons.
2. The QR code should also be reachable from the profile.
3. Wallet and token details looked tappable, but weren't.

Each fix went back into the shared components, so labeled buttons and clear tap targets now apply across the product, not just in the wallet. Next steps: more engaging screens, a web map in the app so people can see where their trees are, and search by token.

## What the system made possible

<GsOutcomes />

### What I learned

In open source, the system is the designer who's always in the room. Writing every rule as a value someone can copy, rather than a principle they have to interpret, is what made it stick with volunteers who come and go.

### Accessibility is cheapest at the token level

Testing color pairings once and publishing the result removed a whole class of review comments. Nobody had to remember to check contrast, because the allowed choices were already safe.
